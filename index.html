About / Methodology

The Homogenometry Dashboard

This tool measures corpus-internal homogeneity by computing pairwise similarity between all responses in your dataset. A dataset may be a collection of student essays, homework exercises, Google Form responses, or any set of text responses you wish to analyze. Rather than comparing against external benchmarks or looking for specific "tells" (like the word "delve" or em dashes), it reveals which responses are unusually similar to each other. Each metric is calculated and displayed separately for transparent interpretation.

Why This Approach?

Traditional detection tools search for specific patterns or "fingerprints" (certain vocabulary, punctuation habits, sentence structures). This approach has limitations:

  • Model-specific: AI fingerprints change with each new model version
  • False positives: Human writers may naturally use flagged words or patterns
  • Narrow focus: Only detects one specific source (e.g., AI) rather than any form of similarity

Homogeneity analysis is different: It doesn't make claims about why responses are similar. It objectively measures pairwise similarity across multiple dimensions of text, identifying pairs showing unusual convergence in vocabulary, phrasing, and structure. This makes it model-agnostic and applicable to any text corpus.

How It Works

The analyzer computes pairwise similarity between all responses using 24 distinct metrics, each capturing different aspects of textual similarity. All metrics can be enabled/disabled and weighted via Metric Settings.

Lexical Similarity Metrics

1. Cosine Similarity - Measures similarity in word frequency patterns by treating each text as a vector of word counts. High scores indicate similar vocabulary usage rates.

Example: Text A: "The cat sat on the mat" (the=2, cat=1, sat=1...) vs Text B: "The dog sat on the rug" (the=2, dog=1, sat=1...) - Similar frequency patterns despite different words.

2. Jaccard Index - Calculates the proportion of shared unique words relative to total unique words. Measures vocabulary overlap without considering word frequency.

Example: Text A uses {education, reform, policy, students} and Text B uses {education, policy, teachers, funding}. Shared: 2 words. Total unique: 6 words. Jaccard = 2/6 = 33.3%.
Character-Level N-gram Metrics

3. N-gram Similarity (3-character) - Compares sequences of 3 consecutive characters, ignoring punctuation. Detects similar spelling patterns and word fragments.

Example: "education" → {edu, duc, uca, cat, ati, tio, ion}. Matches with "educational" which shares most of these 3-grams.

4. N-gram Similarity (4-character) - Same as above but using 4-character sequences for more specificity.

Example: "therefore" → {ther, here, eref, refo, efor, fore}. Catches longer spelling patterns.

5. N-gram Similarity (5-character) - Uses 5-character sequences for even more specific pattern matching.

Example: Identifies overlap in unique phrases or technical terms like "photosynthesis."

6. N-gram with Punctuation - Same as 3-character n-grams but preserves punctuation, capturing formatting and stylistic choices.

Example: "don't" vs "dont" vs "do not" - preserved punctuation reveals exact stylistic matches.
Word-Level N-gram Metrics

7. Word N-gram (3-word phrases) - Identifies shared 3-word sequences.

Example: "In other words" appears in both texts. "The main argument" appears in both.
Fuzzy Matching Metrics (Approximate String Matching)

8. Fuzzy N-gram (3-word phrases, ≤5 character edits) - Compares 3-word phrases allowing up to 5 character differences via Levenshtein distance.

Example: "the primary argument" matches "the main argument" (5 character changes: "primary" → "main").

9. Fuzzy N-gram (6-word phrases, ≤13 character edits) - Longer phrases with more tolerance for changes.

Example: "In conclusion we can see that education" matches "In summary we observe that schooling" (multiple word substitutions within tolerance).

10. Word-Level Fuzzy (3-word phrases, ≤1 word substitution) - Compares 3-word phrases allowing exactly 1 word to differ.

Example: "climate change impacts" matches "climate change effects" (1 word different: impacts → effects).

11. Word-Level Fuzzy (5-word phrases, ≤2 word substitutions) - Longer phrases allowing up to 2 words to differ.

Example: "the study reveals important findings about" matches "this research shows significant results regarding" (2 words changed: study→research, reveals→shows).
Structural Metrics

12. Word Count Similarity - Measures similarity in total word count. Responses with similar length requirements often have similar word counts.

Example: Text A has 247 words, Text B has 251 words. Very similar lengths may indicate similar levels of elaboration or adherence to length requirements.

13. Average Sentence Length - Compares the mean number of words per sentence. Reflects writing style and complexity.

Example: Text A averages 18.3 words/sentence, Text B averages 18.7 words/sentence.

14. Sentence Length Variance - Measures similarity in sentence length variability. Writers with consistent sentence rhythms show similar variance patterns.

Example: Text A has sentence lengths [10, 12, 9, 11] (low variance = consistent). Text B has [8, 22, 5, 19] (high variance = varied). Different writing styles.
Stylistic Metrics

15. Pronoun Density - Compares the proportion of pronouns (I, you, he, she, it, we, they, etc.) in each text. Reflects perspective and voice.

Example: Text A: 12% pronouns (first-person narrative). Text B: 2% pronouns (formal academic).

16. Transition Words Density - Measures usage frequency of transition words and phrases (however, furthermore, for example, in other words, etc.). Reflects rhetorical sophistication and organization.

Example: Text A uses 15 transition phrases in 500 words (3.0%). Text B uses 14 in 480 words (2.9%).
Advanced Metrics

17. Named Entity Recognition (NER) - Identifies and compares proper nouns (people, places, organizations).

Example: Both texts mention "Stanford University," "John Dewey," and "Department of Education."

18. Temporal Burstiness - Analyzes word positioning patterns using the burstiness metric from Cui et al. (2017). Words that appear in concentrated "bursts" vs. evenly distributed show different usage patterns.

Example: Text A uses "democracy" clustered in the introduction (bursty, μ=0.8). Text B uses "democracy" evenly throughout (distributed, μ=0.2).

19. AI Semantic Similarity (Transformers.js) - Uses neural network embeddings to measure semantic meaning similarity independent of vocabulary overlap.

Example: "The economy is improving rapidly" vs. "Economic conditions are getting better quickly" - different words, same meaning. The AI model recognizes these as semantically equivalent (90%+ similarity).

20. Syntactic Template Similarity (Shaib Method) - Analyzes sentence structure using 3-grams of POS tags that approximate Penn Treebank format via Compromise.js to measure syntactic template overlap (Shaib et al. 2024).

Example: "The quick cat jumped" (DET-ADJ-NOUN-VERB) vs. "A fast dog ran" (DET-ADJ-NOUN-VERB) - same syntactic structure pattern.

21. Simple Syntactic Pattern Similarity - Analyzes sentence structure using 3-grams of simple POS tags (NOUN, VERB, ADJECTIVE, etc.) directly from Compromise.js without Penn Treebank approximation.

Example: "The happy dog runs quickly" (Determiner-Adjective-Noun-Verb-Adverb) - simpler tags, easier to interpret.

22. Function Word Distribution (NLP) - Measures similarity in usage of grammatical "glue words" (the, of, and, to, etc.).

Example: Both texts use "the" at 5.1%, "of" at 3.2%, "and" at 2.8%.
Lexical Diversity Metrics

23. Type-Token Ratio (TTR) - Measures lexical diversity as the ratio of unique words (types) to total words (tokens). Higher TTR indicates more varied vocabulary. Similarity is calculated by comparing TTR values between texts.

Example: Text A: "The cat sat on the mat" has 5 unique words / 6 total = TTR of 0.83. Text B with similar vocabulary richness will have a comparable TTR, indicating similar lexical diversity patterns.

24. Moving-Average Type-Token Ratio (MATTR) - A more robust version of TTR that uses a sliding window approach (50-word windows, 10-word stride) to calculate average TTR across the text. Less sensitive to text length than simple TTR.

Example: Instead of one TTR for the entire text, MATTR calculates TTR for each 50-word segment, then averages them. This provides a more stable measure of lexical richness, especially for longer texts.
Libraries & Technologies

Compromise.js - Bundled locally (322 KB). Provides POS tagging and NLP analysis for syntactic patterns and function word detection. No external network request required.

Transformers.js - Downloads AI model from CDN (23 MB, one-time, cached by browser). Uses the Xenova/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model for semantic similarity analysis.

Web Workers - Parallel processing for performance. Metrics that require NLP libraries (Compromise.js) run in the main thread to access the libraries.

Selected metrics are combined two ways: a weighted mean of the raw values, reported as raw similarity, and a double-centred z-score reported in standard deviations, which is what the pair list is ranked and filtered by. Configure which metrics to use and their relative weights via the Metric Settings button in the header menu.

Interpretation

The cohort-relative score asks whether two responses resemble each other more than either resembles the rest of the cohort. Similarity that every response shares - a close-ended prompt, a common source, a shared house style - is absorbed into the row means and subtracted out, so what remains is resemblance specific to those two. A pair with high raw similarity but a low cohort-relative score is usually being driven by the prompt.

This flags pairs for human reading; it does not establish authorship. Adjust the threshold to balance sensitivity and specificity for your use case.

Important Limitations

Scores are dataset-relative, not absolute. Each metric is standardised against the spread of this cohort, so a score states a pair's position within this analysis and cannot be compared across datasets. A score in standard deviations is also not a p-value: every response appears in many pairs, so the values are not independent, and "3 SD" here is a ranking statement rather than a probability.

Double-centring needs enough responses for each row mean to be a stable estimate of how much a response resembles the cohort. Below ten responses it is switched off automatically and metrics are standardised without it; the console reports which mode was used.

Why this matters: A 60% raw similarity in one dataset may be more significant than 75% in another, depending on the natural variance in that corpus. The cohort-relative score exists to absorb exactly that difference, which is why it, and not the percentage, drives the ranking.

Example: Responses to "Define photosynthesis" naturally cluster around similar definitions (high baseline similarity). Responses to "Write a creative short story" show much greater diversity (low baseline). After normalization, both datasets span 0-100%, but the underlying similarity levels are completely different.

Factors affecting score interpretation:

  • Population size: Smaller datasets may have narrower variance ranges, affecting normalization
  • Genre conventions: Academic essays, technical writing, and formulaic responses naturally show higher baseline similarity than creative or open-ended writing
  • Prompt specificity: Highly constrained prompts (e.g., "List the steps of mitosis") produce more homogeneous responses than open prompts
  • Entry length: Shorter responses have fewer opportunities for divergence in vocabulary and structure
  • Skill variance: Homogeneous skill levels (e.g., all advanced writers) produce different score distributions than mixed-ability groups
  • Shared source material: If the assignment requires citing specific texts, legitimate responses will naturally share quoted material and terminology

Best practice: Use similarity scores to compare pairs within the same analysis session, not across different datasets or assignments. Establish context-appropriate thresholds based on the specific assignment type, expected diversity, and your population's characteristics.

Additional limitations:

  • The tool cannot determine why responses are similar (AI use, copying, collaboration, shared sources, or coincidence)
  • Short responses (<50 words) may produce unreliable similarity scores due to limited text for comparison
  • Non-English text may not be properly tokenized, affecting accuracy of word-based metrics
  • Paraphrasing that preserves meaning while changing vocabulary may not be fully detected by lexical metrics
  • Statistical significance: With large datasets, even low similarity scores may represent genuine patterns requiring investigation

Note: This tool is designed for academic integrity research and should be used as one component of a comprehensive evaluation process, not as the sole determinant of academic misconduct.
